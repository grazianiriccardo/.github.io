I study how democratization changed political representation, drawing on the parliamentary record of Belgium between 1880 and 1912.

My fields are political economy and economic history. I combine large-scale text data from parliamentary debates with difference-in-differences designs to study how institutions shape what legislators say and do.

Research

Working papers

Who Speaks for the People? Electoral Reforms, Parliamentary Speech, and Legislative Behavior in Belgium, 1880–1912

with Jakob Vanschoonbeek

Abstract

How do electoral reforms change what legislators talk about and what they do? We study Belgium between 1880 and 1912, when plural suffrage (1893) extended the franchise and proportional representation (1900) changed the electoral system. Using a corpus of about 490,000 parliamentary speeches by 1,231 politicians, and exploiting differences in exposure to the reforms between the Chamber and the Senate, we trace how the reforms reshaped parliamentary attention to labor and social policy, as well as lawmaking in these areas. We then decompose the effects into behavioral and compositional channels. After the introduction of proportional representation, Catholic legislators adapted their behavior within their own careers, whereas changes among Liberals operated mainly through the recomposition of their parliamentary group.
